Part 1 · Understand

Go-to-market for engineers

Go-to-market (GTM) is everything a company does to find customers and turn them into revenue: deciding who to sell to, finding those people, reaching out, and closing deals. A GTM engineer builds the data systems that make that repeatable. The cohort's portfolio project is to do this for one real startup, and Deepu picked Saffron.

Saffron in one paragraph

Saffron (Y Combinator, Spring 2026, San Francisco, 3 founders from Jane Street, MIT and Stanford) sells an AI-native technical interview. A job candidate builds a real feature inside the hiring company's own codebase using Claude Code. Saffron records every prompt and edit, marks each line as written by the human or by AI, and 10+ AI reviewers score the work. The hiring company gets a report in hours without spending any engineer time interviewing. Pricing is public: $199/month for 5 assessments, $499/month for 15, custom for enterprise. Their pitch: the usual engineering interview takes 3–4 weeks, 8+ interviewer hours and over $500 per candidate.

So Saffron's customers are companies that hire software engineers, and the people who buy are CTOs, VPs of Engineering and heads of recruiting. Everything below is about finding those companies and people systematically.

What a GTM engineer does

Sales teams need a steady supply of the right companies to talk to, at the right moment, with the right message. Traditionally a salesperson spent hours per day researching prospects by hand. A GTM engineer automates that research: pull company data from databases, enrich it, detect events that suggest a company needs the product now, qualify or reject each company, find the right person and a working email address, and hand the result to sales or to an outreach tool.

Engineering analogy

It's a data pipeline whose output is qualified sales conversations. Sources are data vendors (Apollo, LinkedIn). Transformations are enrichment and scoring. The sink is an email or LinkedIn outreach tool, or the CRM. Most bugs are at the joins between systems.

The instructor's framing: the job today is about 90% outbound (you reach out to people who haven't contacted you). Data sourcing and cleaning is about 80% of the effort. Clay coined the job title in 2023, and it's now one of the fastest-growing roles in B2B software. People who can code earn noticeably more than pure no-code operators.

The revenue funnel

Every potential customer moves through a series of stages. Each stage has fewer people than the one before, which is why it's drawn as a funnel.

Unknown: everyone in the market who has never heard of you stage 0
Lead: a known contact (you have their name and details) stage 1
MQL: marketing thinks they're a good fit or showed interest stage 2
SQL: sales agrees they're worth a real conversation stage 3
Opportunity: an active deal with a dollar value stage 4
Closed won / closed lost stage 5
Engineering analogy

A state machine with conversion rates between states. Losses compound: if 50% drop at each of four transitions, you keep about 6%. The GTM engineer owns the left side, unknown → lead → first contact, and makes sure replies get routed correctly so nothing is dropped.

MQL = marketing-qualified lead; SQL = sales-qualified lead. The instructor simplifies the end state into three buckets for every company: disqualified (never a fit), nurture (fit, but not now), sell (fit and ready).

ICP and personas: who you sell to

The ICP (Ideal Customer Profile) describes the company that gets the most value from the product. It has three kinds of attributes:

  • Firmographics: facts about the company. Size (employees), industry, location, funding stage, revenue.
  • Technographics: the software they use. For Saffron: do their engineers already use Cursor, Claude Code or Copilot?
  • Situation: what's happening there. For Saffron: are they hiring engineers right now?

A persona is the person inside that company you talk to. There are two kinds:

  • Decision maker: signs off on the purchase. For Saffron: CTO, VP Engineering, Head of Talent Acquisition.
  • Champion: feels the pain daily and pushes internally. For Saffron: engineering managers and technical recruiters.
Engineering analogy

The ICP is a schema plus a WHERE clause: company WHERE employees BETWEEN 50 AND 500 AND country='US' AND industry IN (AI, SaaS, DevTools) AND open_swe_roles >= 5. The persona is the join to the people table: people WHERE title SIMILAR TO ('CTO','VP Engineering'). Everything downstream inherits the quality of this spec, just as a bad schema produces precise garbage.

Two rules from class. Keep job-title keywords and seniority as separate filters (search "engineering" + seniority "VP", not the string "VP of Engineering"). Always add exclude keywords, for example exclude "assistant" or "intern". A tight ICP beats a broad one, because you can write sharper messages and waste fewer credits.

TAM, SAM, SOM: how big the market is

Three nested numbers. Each is a subset of the one before.

TermMeaningSaffron example
TAM
Total Addressable Market		Every company that could theoretically buy.Companies of 50–5,000+ employees in the US, Canada and Europe that regularly hire software engineers.
SAM
Serviceable Addressable Market		The part you can actually reach and serve, given geography, product and price.The same, narrowed to companies that are hiring engineers now and already use AI coding tools.
SOM
Serviceable Obtainable Market		The slice you can realistically win soon. It's an argument about market conditions, not just another filter.US only, 50–500 employees, AI/DevTools/SaaS, Seed to Series C, 5+ open engineering roles. Reason: a 3-person team, self-serve pricing, one decision maker.
Engineering analogy

Successive filters on one dataset, where you read COUNT(*) at each step. GTM engineers don't estimate TAM in dollars from a slide deck. They apply the filters in Apollo and screenshot the result count. The instructor's live example: TAM 151,000 companies → SAM 54,000.

Why it matters

If your ICP returns 40 companies, the market is too small. If it returns 400,000, it's too broad to personalize. The instructor insists that the strategy doc includes the filter screenshots, because clients want evidence rather than claims.

Segments: one message per group

You never write one campaign for the whole market. You split it into 2–3 segments that share a pain, and write a different message for each. Saffron's three segments (in the strategy doc):

S1 · AI-native startups

attack first 50–200 employees. The CTO personally interviews every candidate and engineering time is scarce.

S2 · Growth SaaS / infra

200–1,000 employees. Many interview loops, inconsistent interviewers, slow hiring.

S3 · FinTech / HealthTech

Regulated companies. They need auditable, fair evaluation and worry about take-homes written by ChatGPT.

Engineering analogy

GROUP BY pain_point, then one template per group. Personalization at scale means a sharp template per segment, plus a few per-row variables.

Signals, intent and triggers: why now

Cold outreach becomes warm when there's a reason to reach out this week. The course uses three terms:

Signal
A public fact about the company. Examples: they raised money, they're hiring 12 engineers, a new CTO joined.
Intent
A person interacting with you. Examples: visited your pricing page, liked the founder's LinkedIn post, requested a demo. This is the warmest kind.
Trigger
The action you take in response, such as sending a LinkedIn message referencing the event.
Engineering analogy

Signals and intent are events; triggers are the handlers subscribed to them. Detection cost varies a lot: a job post is cheap to detect, while "which company visited our pricing page" needs a paid visitor-identification tool like RB2B (US only, for legal reasons).

Saffron's top signals, ranked by how easy they are to detect: (1) lots of open engineering roles; (2) recent funding; (3) a new engineering or recruiting leader in the last 180 days; (4) job ads mentioning Cursor or Claude Code; (5) a leader posting about AI changing hiring. The instructor's caveats: funding alone is a weak signal, and you should use a signal as context for your message, never recite it ("we saw you raised money" is off-putting). Verify that a signal is true before relying on it.

Offer and messaging

A good offer follows value → pain → proof. The value is the outcome in the buyer's words. The pain is the problem they already feel. The proof is a reason to believe: a number, a similar customer, or credentials. Then apply the five-word test: would a busy buyer keep reading after the first five words? Openers about yourself ("We are a platform that…") fail. Openers about their world pass.

S1 one-liner: "Your engineers ship with AI. Your interviews don't test it.
Saffron has candidates build a real feature in your codebase with Claude Code,
then scores how they used AI, with zero interviewer hours and results the same day."

Benchmarks the instructor says you can quote honestly: LinkedIn connection acceptance about 70% and replies about 30%; email opens over 90% and replies 3–5%. Plain cold email to a generic list gets about 2% replies, which is why signal-based and LinkedIn-first outreach wins today. Never promise a client open or reply rates.

Data sources and data quality

SourceStrengthWeakness
ApolloHuge company and people database, good filters, generous free tierData goes stale; people-location data lags about 30 days; needs a work email to sign up
ProspeoClean company and people search, department filter, 100 free creditsSmaller free allowance
Sales Navigator (LinkedIn)The freshest "who works where", because people maintain their own profilesNo emails, no API, export needs extra tools; paid (free trial available)
Clay's own searchConvenient inside ClayThe instructor considers its native data less reliable; use it as a supplement

Coverage vs accuracy is the core tradeoff: no single source is right, so you pull from at least three, merge, and dedupe. The dedupe key order taught in class: LinkedIn URL, then email, then full name plus company (never name alone).

Email quality, and why it matters so much

Every email address gets a verification status:

  • Valid: the mailbox exists. Safe to send.
  • Invalid: it bounces. Never send.
  • Valid catch-all: the company's server accepts any address, and a verifier confirmed this one is probably real. Safe to send.
  • Catch-all only: accepted by the server, but nobody may ever read it. Don't send.

Mail providers throttle or block senders whose bounce rate goes above about 2%. A few bad addresses can push your good emails into spam, and you'd blame the copy when the real problem was the data. Also skip companies whose mail server sits behind Mimecast, Proofpoint or Barracuda; these security gateways silently block outreach tools. Expect only about 60% of any raw list to survive cleaning.

Enrichment, waterfalls and "only run if"

Enrichment means adding columns to a row by asking outside services: start with a company name and end up with domain, size, industry, funding, open jobs, decision makers and verified email.

A waterfall tries data provider A. If A finds nothing, it tries B, then C. Order the providers cheapest and most reliable first. You pay per attempt, so a bad order burns credits at scale.

A run condition ("only run if") is a per-row guard. For example, only look for an email if the company qualified, and only verify an email if one was found.

Engineering analogy

A waterfall is a fallback chain: a() ?? b() ?? c(). A run condition is a guard clause: if (!row.qualified) return;. Together they keep cost proportional to the good rows, not to the size of the list.

How Clay works

Clay is a spreadsheet where each column can be a function call: a lookup against 100+ data providers, an AI research agent, or a formula. You import rows, add columns, and run them on selected rows.

Engineering analogy

A dataframe where each column is a lazily evaluated, metered UDF. The meter is credits. Clay has two: data credits (paid to providers and AI models) and actions (Clay's platform fee per operation). A failed lookup still costs, which is why the house rules below exist.

The AI research agent (Claygent) and its model tiers

Clay's AI can browse the web for each row and return a structured answer as JSON. You choose the model per column: Helium (cheapest; fine for single-page lookups), Neon (mid-tier; multi-page), Argon (most thorough and most expensive). Always start with Helium, and escalate only the rows where it failed.

Clay house rules from the instructor

1. Turn auto-run off right after creating a table. Otherwise every edit re-runs and re-charges columns.

2. Hide columns, never delete them. Deleted results are gone, or cost about 50 credits to recover.

3. Test every column on 10 rows before running all 50.

4. Use free formulas for anything deterministic; use AI only for real research.

5. After editing an AI prompt, click Generate JSON schema again. Never hand-delete the output fields.

6. Lock column names before writing run conditions. Renaming a column later silently breaks every condition that references it.

The bonus point (the LinkedIn post topic)

Credit efficiency comes from three levers used together: (1) the cheapest model that can do the job, escalating only on failure; (2) "only run if" gates, so expensive columns run only on qualified rows; (3) testing on 10 rows before scaling. Part 2 has you record credit usage so the post can quote real numbers.

The seven layers (the course's map)

The whole 12-week course is organized around these layers. The cohort has covered L1, L2 and part of L3 and L7 so far.

L1

Data & sourcing: where prospects come from. Apollo, Prospeo, Sales Navigator. Fails when: you target the wrong companies.

L2

Enrichment: turn a name into a verified email and context. Clay waterfalls, email verifiers. Fails when: guessed emails bounce and burn your domain.

L3

Signals & intent: the "why now". Job posts, funding, leadership changes, RB2B. Fails when: the signal is stale or false.

L4

Orchestration: move data between tools. n8n, Make, webhooks. Fails when: a webhook silently drops rows.

L5

Execution: the outreach itself. Instantly, HeyReach, Lemlist. Fails when: domains aren't warmed or the copy is generic.

L6

CRM & reporting: the source of truth. HubSpot, Salesforce, Attio. Fails when: duplicates make reports lie.

L7

AI & agents: research, copy, scripts. Claude, Claude Code, Claygent. Fails when: AI produces confident nonsense at scale with no checks.

The key idea: value is created inside the layers and destroyed at the handoffs between them.

Glossary

B2B
Business-to-business: selling to companies rather than consumers.
Outbound / inbound
Outbound: you contact prospects first. Inbound: they come to you (ads, content, search).
Account
A company. "Account list" means a list of companies, not people.
Lead / contact
A person at an account.
Firmographics / technographics
Company facts / the company's software stack.
Qualification
Deciding whether an account is worth reaching out to. In this course it's binary: Outreach or Don't outreach, with a written reason.
Blacklist / exclusion list
Never contact (competitors) / don't contact for now (current customers, open deals).
Enrichment
Adding data to a row from outside sources.
Waterfall
Trying providers one after another until one returns a result.
Catch-all
A mail server that accepts any address, so plain verification can't confirm a person exists.
MX record
DNS record naming a domain's mail server. Used to skip Mimecast/Proofpoint-protected companies.
SPF / DKIM / DMARC
DNS records that prove your emails are really from your domain. The instructor calls email infrastructure 80% of email success.
Sequencer
A tool that sends multi-step email or LinkedIn campaigns automatically (Instantly, HeyReach).
CRM
Customer relationship management system: the database of accounts, contacts and deals (HubSpot, Salesforce).
Credits / actions
Clay's two usage meters: provider and AI cost / platform operations.
Claygent · Helium / Neon / Argon
Clay's web-research AI and its model tiers, from cheapest to most thorough.
Sculptor
Clay's assistant that answers questions about your table. Keep it in Analyze mode; Build mode edits the table.
Loom
Screen-recording tool. The portfolio needs 5 Loom walkthroughs.
