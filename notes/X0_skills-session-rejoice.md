# Rejoice session: Skills needed to crack future GTM engineering roles (2026-08-15)

## TL;DR
- The market has split into "tool operators" (who just run Clay/coding agents) and "revenue engineers" (who use those tools to produce a business outcome) — only the second group is durable.
- Rejoice's stack of five non-negotiable skills, in order of priority: GTM fundamentals → system thinking → technical fluency → AI/automation tooling → communication. Tools come fourth on purpose.
- The best Clay build she ever shipped had just **one enrichment column** — complexity is not the goal, solving the actual business problem is.
- Communication is described as "the cheat code": her own path into a top agency and into the Clay Cup round-of-32 was won largely on how clearly she explained a build, not on the build's complexity.
- Career advice: don't apply cold with a resume — build a project, record a Loom of it, and DM the founder directly; document the whole learning journey publicly on LinkedIn for credibility/leverage.
- Worked example: a "buying-window detection" system for security company Vanta, combining a trigger signal (leadership hires), two leading signals (hiring momentum, expansion activity), AI-synthesized analysis, and a scored "why now" output per company.

## Who the speaker is
Rejoice Onyenanu — GTM engineer, under one year full-time in the industry (started September of the prior year), currently at agency **Understory** (one of the "elite" GTM agencies); reached round 3 of the Clay Cup competition.

## Key insights (grouped by theme)

**The role**
- Two archetypes exist: tool operators vs. revenue engineers. Tools change constantly (Clay, cloud/coding agents, Prospeo, etc.); the ability to convert a tool into a revenue outcome does not.
- Job descriptions have shifted from "can you use Clay/HubSpot" to "what have you built" — hiring managers and agencies routinely skip resumes and ask for a Loom video instead.
- One well-targeted enrichment column can outperform a sprawling 20-column build; scope the build to the specific business problem, not to signal-collecting.

**Skills / tools**
1. **GTM fundamentals** (buyer journeys, personas, ICP, revenue models, funnel segmentation) — the foundation; without it nothing else holds. Recommended: spend ~1 month just reading before touching any tool.
2. **System thinking** — map the full process (data source → clean → enrich → route → sequence → handle failure) *before* opening Clay. A system is something that "carries revenue forward," not just automation for its own sake. Practiced, not watched — there's no course for it, only repeated building.
3. **Technical fluency** — enough understanding of APIs, HTTP, JSON, and data structures to debug your own build; not software-engineering depth.
4. **AI + automation** — Clay, cloud/coding agents, GPT, prompting skill. Explicitly ranked fourth, after strategy and systems, not first.
5. **Communication** — described as the most important differentiator and "a cheat code." Learnable regardless of introvert/extrovert disposition, via deliberate practice (watching communication videos, structuring build explanations like a story, color-coding table columns by function).

**AI & coding agents**
- Building inside Clay itself is becoming less necessary at some companies — some no longer touch Clay and build everything through coding agents instead.
- What won't get automated away: the GTM *strategist* — the person who understands segmentation, ICP, buyer journey, and can design the revenue system, as opposed to just operating a tool.
- Clay itself isn't going away, but its center of gravity is shifting toward enterprise; individual operators are increasingly moving to coding agents and other tools (mentioned: "ref code" as a Clay alternative that integrates with coding agents).

**Hiring & portfolio**
- Loom videos of real builds routinely replace resumes in this industry; 132 people applied to the Clay Cup round she competed in, 32 advanced — the deciding factor was largely the quality of the 5-minute build explanation, not raw technical complexity.
- Portfolio rule of thumb: "your portfolio should make the hiring manager feel like they've already watched you do the job."
- Tactic for a project with no real client: take a target company's job description, feed it to AI to generate a project idea that "proves you could do the job," build it, then pitch the founder directly (this is literally how she got noticed by Active Campaign).
- Personal brand on LinkedIn is treated as leverage that substitutes for lack of experience — document wins *and* failures/broken workflows publicly; this builds credibility with agencies and can itself generate inbound leads for them.

**Niches / learning approach**
- Recommended entry path: start in outbound (via an agency), because it exposes you to full infrastructure, many industries, and end-to-end client communication — specialize only after you've "eaten the whole cake."
- Self-teaching for non-sales backgrounds: read GTM blogs/content for about a month before touching tools; system thinking develops from repeated building, not from a single course.
- For personalization-level decisions: work backward from what the ideal, fully personalized email would look like, break it into variables, then figure out what enrichment is needed to fill each variable — rather than starting from "what can Clay enrich" and personalizing outward.
- For a "revenue leak" or "buying window" type of build: first define precisely what that concept *means* for the specific company/product before choosing any signal or enrichment.

## Practical advice to act on (checklist)
- [ ] Spend real time (weeks, not hours) on GTM fundamentals — buyer journeys, ICP, personas, funnel/segmentation — before building anything in Clay.
- [ ] Before opening a tool, write down the process end-to-end: data source → clean → enrich → route → sequence → failure handling.
- [ ] Build 3–5 small personal/portfolio projects (even without a real client), each solving one clearly stated business problem, and save them with an explanation.
- [ ] Record a Loom for every project explaining it "like a story" — what you were solving, why, and how.
- [ ] Post the build (and its failures) on LinkedIn regularly instead of waiting until it's polished.
- [ ] When applying/pitching, skip generic applications — send a targeted Loom + built artifact directly to the founder/hiring manager.
- [ ] Practice communication deliberately (watch talks on it, rehearse explaining a build) — treat it as a trainable skill, not an innate trait.
